A power station for Starlink is one of the most popular reasons people buy a portable battery — remote workers, RV travelers and anyone who wants internet during a power outage. Starlink is power-hungry compared with a home router, so it pays to size the battery correctly.

## How much power Starlink uses

Power use depends on which kit you have and what it's doing:

| Kit | Typical average | Peaks |
|---|---|---|
| Starlink Standard | about 75–100 W | around 150 W, more with snow melt |
| Starlink Mini | about 25–50 W | around 75 W |

These are commonly reported figures, not a guarantee. Use the power reading in the Starlink app for your own dish — it is the most accurate number you'll get.

The key difference: a home Wi-Fi router uses around 20 W. A Standard Starlink dish can use **four to five times** as much, around the clock.

## How long a power station will run Starlink

Runtime is roughly *capacity × efficiency ÷ average watts*. We use 0.85 for the AC outlet and 0.9 for DC power, which the Mini can use.

| Power station | Standard (≈85 W, AC) | Mini (≈40 W, DC) |
|---|---|---|
| 500 Wh | about 5 hours | about 11 hours |
| 1,000 Wh | about 10 hours | about 22 hours |
| 2,000 Wh | about 20 hours | about 45 hours |

## What size you need for a full day

Take daily use, allow for losses, and add a 20% buffer:

- **Standard, 24 hours:** 85 W × 24 h ≈ 2,040 Wh → about **2,900 Wh** of power station.
- **Mini, 24 hours on DC:** 40 W × 24 h ≈ 960 Wh → about **1,300 Wh**.
- **Working hours only (8 h):** Standard needs about 1,000 Wh; Mini about 450 Wh.

Most people don't need Starlink all night. Sizing for the hours you actually use it can halve the battery you need.

## Standard vs Mini on battery

If you plan to run on battery often — camping, van life or long outages — the **Mini** is far easier to power. It uses less energy and runs on DC, so you skip the inverter losses that come with an AC outlet. The Standard kit plugs into AC through its power supply, so plan for the AC efficiency loss.

## Adding solar

On a sunny day, solar panels can carry Starlink. A 200 W panel set in 5 hours of good sun makes about **750 Wh** after real-world losses — close to a full day for a Mini, or most of a working day for a Standard dish. Run the numbers for your location with the solar charging time calculator.

## Tips to stretch your battery

- **Switch it off** when you're not online. Starlink keeps drawing power while idle.
- **Use power-saving or sleep schedules** in the Starlink app if your kit offers them.
- **Skip snow-melt mode** unless you actually have snow on the dish.
- **Use DC for the Mini** with a port and cable rated for enough watts.
- **Keep the power station out of direct sun** — batteries and electronics work best when they stay cool.

## Before you buy

Check three specs: enough **Wh** for your hours, enough **continuous output** (the Standard needs at least around 150–200 W to cover peaks comfortably), and the right **ports** — an AC outlet for the Standard, or a high-wattage USB-C or DC output for the Mini.
