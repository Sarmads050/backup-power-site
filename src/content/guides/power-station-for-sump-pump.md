A power station for a sump pump can be the difference between a dry basement and a flooded one when a storm knocks out the power. But sump pumps are demanding: they need a big burst of power to start, and in heavy rain they may run again and again. Here's how to size one properly.

## How much power a sump pump needs

Sump pumps are motors, so they have two numbers that matter:

- **Running watts** — what the pump draws while pumping.
- **Starting watts** — the short surge, often well above running watts, when the motor kicks in.

| Pump size | Running watts (approx.) | Starting watts (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| 1/3 HP | about 800 W | about 1,300 W |
| 1/2 HP | about 1,050 W | about 2,150 W |

Your pump's label is more accurate than any chart: **amps × 120 V = running watts**.

The rule is simple: your power station's **surge rating must be higher than the pump's starting watts**, and its **continuous output must be higher than the running watts**. If not, it will shut off the instant the pump tries to start — exactly when you need it.

## Duty cycle: the number that decides battery size

A sump pump doesn't run all the time. It switches on when water reaches a set level, empties the pit, then stops. The share of time it runs is its **duty cycle**, and it changes completely with the weather.

For a 1/3 HP pump (800 W) over 24 hours:

- **Running 10% of the time:** 800 × 24 × 0.10 = **1,920 Wh per day**
- **Running 30% of the time:** 800 × 24 × 0.30 = **5,760 Wh per day**
- **Heavy storm, running almost constantly:** close to **19,200 Wh per day** — far beyond a portable battery.

## Turning that into a power station size

Divide the daily energy by 0.85 (about 15% is lost turning battery power into AC power) and add a 20% safety buffer:

- 1,920 Wh per day → about **2,700 Wh** of capacity for one day
- 5,760 Wh per day → about **8,100 Wh** of capacity for one day

The first is a large portable power station. The second is home-battery territory, usually an expandable system with extra battery packs.

## How to measure your own pump

You can't size a battery without knowing how hard your pump works:

1. **Time it during heavy rain.** With a stopwatch, note how many minutes the pump runs in an hour. Ten minutes an hour is about a 17% duty cycle.
2. **Use a plug-in watt meter.** Plug the pump into one for a rainy day and read the total watt-hours.
3. **Watch the pit level** during your next storm to see how fast it refills.

Do this before you buy — the answer could mean the difference between a mid-size and a whole-home system.

## Alternatives worth considering

- **Battery backup sump pump systems.** A second, battery-powered pump sits in the pit and switches on automatically when the main pump loses power. It's purpose-built for this one job.
- **Water-powered backup pumps.** Where household water pressure allows, these use city water to pump out the pit with no electricity at all.
- **A gas generator outdoors.** For multi-day storms with a busy pump, a generator can keep going as long as you have fuel — but it must run outdoors, well away from the house, because of carbon monoxide.

Many homeowners combine options: a backup pump for flooding and a power station for the fridge, lights and phones.

## Setting it up safely

- **Pure sine wave output** is recommended for motors; most modern power stations provide it.
- **Plug-in pumps:** plug directly into the power station, or use a heavy-duty extension cord rated for at least the pump's amps. Keep cords and the power station well above any water.
- **Hardwired pumps** need a licensed electrician and a proper transfer switch — never improvise a connection to your home's wiring.
- **Test before storm season.** Unplug the pump from the wall, plug it into the power station and pour water into the pit to check it starts every time.
- **Keep the power station charged** and top it up every few months.
