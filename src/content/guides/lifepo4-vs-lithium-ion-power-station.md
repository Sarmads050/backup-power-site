The LiFePO4 vs lithium-ion power station question confuses a lot of buyers, partly because both are actually lithium-ion. The real choice is between two chemistries — and for most home backup, one is the clear winner.

## Two kinds of lithium-ion

"Lithium-ion" is a family of batteries. Portable power stations mostly use one of two types:

- **LiFePO4** — lithium iron phosphate, often shortened to **LFP**.
- **NMC** — nickel manganese cobalt, the type found in many laptops and older power stations.

The spec sheet usually lists the chemistry under "cell type" or "battery chemistry." If it says LiFePO4 or LFP, you know what you're getting. If it only says "lithium-ion," it is often NMC — check the cycle rating to be sure.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | LiFePO4 (LFP) | NMC lithium-ion |
|---|---|---|
| Typical cycle rating | about 3,000–4,000 cycles to ~80% capacity | about 500–1,000 cycles |
| Weight for the same Wh | heavier, bulkier | lighter, smaller |
| Heat stability | more stable, less prone to overheating | less stable (still protected by a BMS) |
| Best for | home backup, frequent use, long life | occasional, weight-sensitive use |

One **cycle** is one full charge and discharge — or two half ones.

## Lifespan: the biggest difference

Cycle life is where LiFePO4 pulls far ahead. A unit rated for **3,000 cycles** could be fully drained and recharged every day for more than eight years. An NMC unit rated for **800 cycles** reaches the same point in a little over two years of daily use.

For a power station that only comes out during storms, you may never hit either limit — but if you also use it for camping, an RV or daily solar, LiFePO4's extra cycles matter a lot.

## The real cost: price per cycle

Sticker price can mislead. Divide price by rated cycles to compare fairly:

- A **$1,000** unit rated for **3,000 cycles** costs about **$0.33 per cycle**.
- A **$700** unit rated for **800 cycles** costs about **$0.88 per cycle**.

The cheaper box can be the more expensive battery. LiFePO4 used to cost noticeably more up front, but it is now the most common chemistry in new power stations, so the gap has largely closed.

## Weight and size

NMC packs more energy into each pound, so an NMC power station is lighter and smaller than a LiFePO4 one with the same watt-hours. That matters if you're carrying it up a trail or packing a car for travel. For a unit that lives in a closet and rolls out during an outage, the extra weight of LiFePO4 rarely matters.

## Safety and heat

Both types include a **battery management system (BMS)** that protects against overcharging, short circuits and overheating. LiFePO4 chemistry itself is more **thermally stable**, meaning it is less prone to overheating if something goes wrong. For a battery that sits inside your home for years, that's a real advantage.

## Cold weather and storage

The rules here apply to both chemistries:

- **Don't charge below freezing.** Most lithium batteries shouldn't be charged below **32°F (0°C)** unless they have built-in heating. Usable capacity also drops in the cold.
- **Store it partly charged.** Around **50–80%** is common advice unless your manual says otherwise.
- **Top it up every 3–6 months** so it's ready when the power goes out.
- **Keep it cool and dry**, away from direct sun and damp garages.

## Which should you choose?

- **Home backup, frequent use or solar charging:** choose **LiFePO4**. Longer life, better heat stability and a lower cost per cycle.
- **Occasional ultralight use** where every pound counts: **NMC** can make sense.
