Choosing a power station for a refrigerator comes down to two questions: can it start the compressor, and can it keep it running for as long as your outage lasts? Both have clear answers once you know a few numbers about your fridge.

## How much power a refrigerator actually uses

A fridge doesn't run flat-out all day. Its compressor switches on, cools the box, then switches off again. While it is running, a typical modern full-size refrigerator draws roughly **100–250 W**. Because it only runs for about a third of the day, the energy it uses adds up to around **1,100–1,900 Wh per day** for most full-size models.

The number that catches people out is the **start-up surge**. Like any motor, a compressor pulls two to three times its running watts for a second or two as it starts — often around **600–1,200 W**. If your power station can't deliver that burst, it will shut off every time the fridge tries to cool.

### Find your own fridge's numbers

- **EnergyGuide label:** the yellow sticker shows kWh per year. Divide by 365 and multiply by 1,000 to get Wh per day. A fridge rated 580 kWh/year uses about 1,590 Wh a day.
- **Watt meter:** plug the fridge into an inexpensive plug-in watt meter for 24 hours. It shows the real watt-hours used — the most accurate number you can get.
- **Spec label inside the fridge:** shows amps. Multiply by 120 for an idea of the running watts.

## What size power station you need

Use three specs on the box: capacity (Wh), continuous output (W) and surge (W).

1. **Capacity:** take your daily Wh, divide by 0.85 (about 15% is lost turning battery power into normal AC power) and add a 20% safety buffer for cold weather, battery ageing and doors being opened. A fridge using about 1,580 Wh a day needs roughly **2,200–2,300 Wh** for one full day.
2. **Continuous output:** the fridge alone needs only a few hundred watts — look for **at least 300 W**, more if you'll run lights and Wi-Fi too.
3. **Surge:** at least **1,200 W**, so the compressor can start every time.

## How long common sizes will run a fridge

Runtime is roughly *capacity × 0.85 ÷ average watts*. A fridge using 1,580 Wh a day averages about 66 W across 24 hours:

| Power station capacity | Approximate fridge runtime |
|---|---|
| 500 Wh | about 6 hours |
| 1,000 Wh | about 13 hours |
| 2,000 Wh | about 26 hours |
| 3,600 Wh | about 46 hours |

These are estimates for a typical full-size fridge. An older or larger model, a hot kitchen or frequent door opening will shorten them. A mini fridge (about 80 W while running) or a chest freezer (about 100 W while running) uses much less.

## How long your food stays safe without any power

You may not need to run the fridge non-stop. According to USDA food-safety guidance, an **unopened refrigerator keeps food safe for about 4 hours** without power. A **full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours** (about 24 hours if half full) if the door stays closed. That gives you room to plan.

## Ways to make your battery last longer

- **Keep the door closed.** Every opening lets cold air out and makes the compressor work harder.
- **Set it a little warmer** within the safe range of 37–40°F instead of the coldest setting.
- **Run it in bursts.** Power the fridge for a few hours, then unplug it for a few hours, checking the temperature with a fridge thermometer. This can stretch a smaller battery much further.
- **Pre-chill before a storm.** Turn the fridge and freezer colder a day ahead and fill empty space with jugs of water, which hold cold.
- **Add solar.** A few hundred watts of panels can put back much of what the fridge uses on a sunny day — try the solar charging time calculator to see how much.

## Other things to check before you buy

- **Pure sine wave output.** Compressors need clean power; nearly all modern power stations provide it, but confirm on the spec sheet.
- **Battery type.** For home backup that sits ready for years, a LiFePO4 battery is usually the better choice because it lasts far more charge cycles.
- **Everything else on the circuit.** Lights, phones, Wi-Fi and a fan all add to your daily total.
